The rule is a ratio, so it does not depend on the size of the account and does not need restating as the account grows or shrinks. At 1.500× the position is 1.50× equity against a loan of 0.50× equity, and the financing costs 2.67% of equity a year. A margin call arrives after a −55.6% decline; equity reaches zero at −66.7%. Those three figures hold at any account size.

Leverage drifts as L′ = L(1+x) / (1+Lx), so from 1.500× a 16% fall reaches the upper bound and a 31% rally reaches the lower one. Both bounds bind in practice, which means the rule sells after declines and borrows more after rallies — the second half is the part worth deciding on deliberately.

Stated as one sentence: the rule roughly doubles the median outcome, gives up about a quarter of the bad-case outcome, and makes a 70% drawdown twelve times more likely.

Whether that is a good trade is not a question the data answers — it is the question the data is for. Note which side of it is certain. The financing cost and the deeper drawdowns arrive regardless. The doubled median depends on an expected return that carries a 1.9-point standard error, and a 2-point shortfall would take the better half of the gain with it.

The figure that governs the decision

37%. Roughly one chance in three of watching the account fall more than 70% at some point over forty years. At the fifth percentile of paths, the worst moment leaves 36 cents for every dollar deposited. No return figure in the table above survives an investor who sells there.

No path in three thousand was wiped out. That is not reassurance — it is a property of rebalancing to a constant target, which cuts the position as equity falls. Ruin needs a single-day gap worse than −1/L, about −67% here, and the worst day in a century of data is −19%. Leverage at this size does not bankrupt an account. It ruins one.

Why there is no third decimal

The target was asked for three times to three decimal places. This is the answer.

On a 0.025 grid, maximising CRRA expected utility at risk aversion 1.5 does peak at 1.500× — that figure is an argmax, not a rounding. But the same computation reports its own noise: the Monte Carlo standard error on expected utility is 24% of the entire utility range across 1.0× to 2.0×.

Three decimals are unavailable, and the first decimal is barely identified. That measurement also settles the band, which two earlier attempts left unresolved — its utility optimum moved from 5.9% to 2.0% purely on which reconstruction of history was used, which is noise, not signal.

So the band is set to match the target's confidence set instead, on a principle that needs no fitting: holding a position to a tighter tolerance than the target is identified to is pure transaction cost. A ±2% band trades 560 times in forty years against 19, and buys 0.2 points of median CAGR that is itself inside the noise.

The answer moved four times

Each revision superseded the last. Three were caught by a check; one by an outside reviewer.

01

Ranked on a sample with no bear market

SPMO launched in October 2015. Its own record contains no 2008, no 2000–02 and no 1929 — the events that decide whether leverage works. Every criterion run on that decade said borrow the maximum the broker allows.

3.0× → 1.475×

02

Three biases, all pointing the same way

An in-sample alpha with a t-statistic of 1.16 projected across a century; 29% of variance discarded when synthesising history; financing under-charged by 30% through a day-count error. In a backtest the errors are rarely randomly signed.

1.475× → 1.0×

03

The holding period was never stated as a choice

Everything had been answered on ten years. The optimum does not move with horizon, but the price of sitting above it collapses: a bad decade at 1.5× loses money, while a bad forty years still compounds above 5%.

1.0× → 1.5×

04

An independent review found two more real bugs

A total wipeout was recorded as a flat day, so ruined paths reported no drawdown at all. The drawdown-constrained optimiser returned the largest feasible leverage rather than the growth maximum. Both were confirmed independently; the equivalence test had a coverage hole exactly where the first bug lived.

1.5× → 1.500× with a measured confidence set

What would overturn it

In order of how much they move the answer.

A broker-entity surcharge — check this first

IBKR's Australian entity adds 2% to non-AUD borrowing for retail clients and for non-retail natural persons on a Standard Margin Lending Facility, applied to every tier. At 7.13% financing the target falls to roughly 1.2×. Read the rate off a statement, not off the website.

Expected return being lower than estimated

This is the binding uncertainty, not grid precision. A persistent 2 point shortfall moves the best tested leverage to 1.75× on log growth; 4 points moves it to 1.25×. The standard error on the mean return over a century is about 1.9 points, so both are ordinary outcomes.

Forward volatility above its historical level

Kelly scales with 1/σ². Long-only momentum ran at 18.9% volatility over the century and 23.5% in the 2020s; at the latter the target collapses toward 1.0×. Volatility is forecastable in a way returns are not, which is why it is worth extrapolating and returns are not.

A shorter horizon than forty years

The figure is not transferable to money that might be needed sooner — and "needed sooner" includes being frightened out at a bottom.

Momentum's factor premium not persisting

Zeroing the fund's own alpha still credits about 2.1%/yr of momentum premium. Removing it while keeping the risk takes full Kelly from 2.15× to 1.66×.

Still unresolved

Named rather than fixed.

89.6% of the history is not SPMO. Two reconstructions were built — one from market and momentum factor loadings, one from the measured returns of a real long-only large-cap momentum portfolio since 1926 — and they agree on the target to within 3%. But they correlate at 0.873 and share the same live segment, so they are two constructions, not independent evidence. Neither can say how a momentum-screened S&P 500 fund would have behaved in 1932, and both understate the drawdown: 41–50% through the technology bubble against the published index's 58.61%.

The ticker was selected because it did well. Running the identical pipeline across five surviving momentum funds gives targets from 1.0× to 1.5×, so the result is not unique to the winner — but it is not general either, and nothing inside a model corrects for selection at the moment the ticker was chosen.

Fitting did not beat a fixed rule out of sample. Over 2019–2026, fixed leverage beat annually refitted selection on all five funds. That is a reason to operate a fixed rule rather than re-optimise, and equally a reason to doubt that the fitting identifies anything.
